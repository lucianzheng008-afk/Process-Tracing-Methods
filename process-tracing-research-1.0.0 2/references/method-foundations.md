# Method foundations

## What process tracing can infer

Process tracing asks whether and how a causal process operated inside a bounded case. Its distinctive evidence concerns the fingerprints of activities linking an initial condition to an outcome. Cross-case association can inform priors and case choice, but it does not substitute for within-case mechanistic evidence.

Separate three questions:

1. **Occurrence:** did a proposed process operate in this case?
2. **Production:** how did entities and activities transmit causal influence from C to O?
3. **Contribution:** which parts of the outcome can this process account for, relative to alternatives?

The method does not by itself establish average effects, population frequency, or the counterfactual difference made by a nonexclusive mechanism. State the inferential target precisely.

## Four designs

### Theory-testing

C, O, and a plausible mechanism are specified. The analyst predicts fingerprints, then evaluates whether each mechanism part operated. Confirmation establishes bounded operation, not universal validity or relative effect size.

### Theory-building

C and O are known but the link is not. Narrative, theory, and surprising observations are used abductively to develop a potentially transferable mechanism. Separate discovery material from later tests.

### Theory-revision

A mechanism worked in a typical case but fails in a C+/O− case. Trace until breakdown, then compare the deviant and typical cases to identify an omitted contextual or causal condition. Process tracing focuses the comparison; it does not replace it.

### Explaining-outcome

The target is a singular named event. Combine compatible theories and case-specific factors into a minimally sufficient explanation. The result is case-centric; generalizable elements may be proposed but not presumed.

## Two mechanism resolutions

**Minimalist:** the mechanism remains partly a gray box. Suitable for early plausibility probes or outward checks when resources are limited. Inference is correspondingly weak.

**Systems:** the mechanism is decomposed into entities engaging in activities, connected by productive continuity. Suitable when the question asks how the process worked and the record can reveal multiple parts.

Choose resolution by inferential aim and evidence, not by aspiration. A systems diagram with no observable implications is decoration.

## Mechanistic evidence

An observation becomes evidence only after two evaluations:

1. **Theory stage:** would the proposed activity produce this fingerprint, and could alternatives also produce it?
2. **Empirical stage:** does the source actually establish the observation, and can the observation be trusted and interpreted in context?

Keep these stages separate. “Official” may improve authenticity but not uniqueness. “Private” may reduce audience incentives but does not ensure candor or centrality.

## Test labels

The familiar labels summarize combinations of certainty and uniqueness:

| Test shorthand | If found | If absent |
|---|---|---|
| Straw in the wind | small confirmation | small disconfirmation |
| Hoop | little confirmation | potentially strong disconfirmation |
| Smoking gun | potentially strong confirmation | little disconfirmation |
| Doubly decisive | strong confirmation | strong disconfirmation |

Never assign a label from surface form. The same document can be a hoop for one proposition and weak straw for another. Translate every label into: `How likely is E under H? How likely is E under the best alternative? Would E have been observable if H were true?`

## Causal humility

- Mechanisms can be contributing rather than necessary or sufficient.
- Multiple mechanisms can operate together.
- Evidence supporting H may not disconfirm H2.
- The mechanism-level conclusion is capped by the weakest necessary part.
- An absence is evidence only when occurrence would reliably leave an accessible trace.
- Generalization requires repeated study across contextual differences; it is never an automatic reward for detail.
